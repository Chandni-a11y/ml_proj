CONCLUSION

This project investigated whether a machine learning system can
recognize situations in which its own prediction may not be reliable.

A Logistic Regression classifier was first developed as a baseline.
It achieved 98.6% accuracy on the original test set. The model's
prediction confidence was then analyzed to investigate whether
confidence could act as a reliability signal.

The experiments showed that correct predictions had higher average
confidence than wrong predictions. An abstention mechanism was also
tested by applying different confidence thresholds. At a 90%
threshold, 125 of 143 predictions were accepted automatically and
both errors in the test set were flagged for review.

However, the experiments also showed an important limitation.
Under a controlled distribution shift, model accuracy decreased from
98.6% to 95.1%, while average confidence remained high. This indicates
that high confidence does not always indicate that the model is
reliable when the input data changes.

The calibration experiment also showed that sigmoid calibration did
not improve the Brier Score on this particular test split.

Overall, the experiments provide initial evidence that confidence-based
abstention can help identify some potentially unreliable predictions.
However, confidence alone is not sufficient to fully determine whether
a prediction should be trusted, particularly under distribution shift.

Further research should investigate stronger uncertainty estimation,
out-of-distribution detection, additional calibration methods, and
more realistic distribution shifts.
