FINDINGS

1. Baseline Model

The baseline Logistic Regression model achieved an accuracy of 98.6%
on the test set, with 143 testing samples. The model made 2 incorrect
predictions.

This shows that the model performs well on the original test data, but
accuracy alone does not indicate whether the model knows when its
prediction may be unreliable.


2. Confidence Analysis

The average confidence of correct predictions was 96.04%, while the
average confidence of wrong predictions was 75.49%.

This suggests that confidence can provide useful information about
prediction reliability. However, wrong predictions can still have
relatively high confidence, so confidence is not a perfect reliability
measure.


3. Calibration

The original model had a Brier Score of 0.0181, while the sigmoid
calibrated model had a Brier Score of 0.0273.

Since a lower Brier Score indicates better probabilistic performance,
the original model performed better on this test split according to
this metric.

Therefore, sigmoid calibration did not improve the result in this
particular experiment. This does not mean that calibration methods are
generally ineffective; different methods, datasets, and evaluation
settings may produce different results.


4. Distribution Shift

When one feature in the test data was increased by 50%, the model's
accuracy decreased from 98.6% to 95.1%.

However, average confidence remained high, changing only from 95.75%
to 94.96%.

This indicates that model confidence did not decrease proportionally
to the performance degradation. Therefore, high confidence alone may
not be sufficient for identifying changed or unfamiliar data.


5. Abstention

The abstention experiment tested different confidence thresholds.

At a 90% confidence threshold, 125 out of 143 predictions were
accepted automatically and 18 were flagged for review.

All 125 accepted predictions were correct, while both model errors were
flagged for review. This produced an error rejection rate of 100% on
this particular test split.

This provides initial evidence that an abstention mechanism can help
reduce automatic errors by sending lower-confidence predictions for
human review.


6. Reliability Under Distribution Shift

On the shifted test data, the 90% threshold accepted 119 predictions,
giving a coverage of 83.22%.

The accepted predictions had 100% accuracy on this particular shifted
test split.

However, the distribution shift was created artificially by modifying
one feature. Therefore, this experiment should be considered a
controlled robustness experiment rather than a complete real-world
out-of-distribution evaluation.


7. Confidence vs Actual Accuracy

The confidence-bin experiment showed that the confidence ranges did
not produce a perfectly increasing accuracy pattern.

Several confidence ranges contained very few samples. For example,
the 70%-80% range contained only one prediction.

Therefore, these results provide preliminary evidence about the
relationship between confidence and correctness, but they are not
sufficient to make a strong calibration conclusion.


OVERALL FINDING

The experiments suggest that confidence can be useful as a reliability
signal and that abstention can help identify predictions that should
receive human review.

However, confidence alone does not completely solve the reliability
problem, especially when the input data changes.

The results therefore support further investigation of uncertainty
estimation and out-of-distribution detection methods.
